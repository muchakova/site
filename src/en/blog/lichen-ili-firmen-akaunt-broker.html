What changes when we read the law letter by letter. Up to date as of 8 October 2026.

The choice between investing as an individual or through a company usually boils down to “where will I pay less tax”. The right question is a different one: which law applies, what exactly does it say, and what can I prove. The Personal Income Tax Act (PITA) and the Corporate Income Tax Act (CITA) treat the same transactions differently – sometimes in the company’s favour, sometimes not. And the difference between the two regimes often comes down to details that look technical: the exchange segment, the MIC code, the type of instrument.

A frequently asked question

Question: “How do I turn off smart routing in Trading 212?”

Under a post asking this, I came across the following comment (translated from Bulgarian):

“A while ago Gemini told me that if the share or ETF is listed on a regulated European exchange (for example Xetra in Frankfurt), the income from the sale is exempt from the 10% tax. When declaring to the NRA (in Appendix No. 5), what counts is the ISIN code and the exchange where the security is listed. I keep the monthly and annual statements from the broker, which clearly show the ISIN of the security and the European exchange, so that if the NRA asks I can confirm the status of the instrument. No problems so far, but I can’t guarantee that’s how it is”

The comment is emblematic. Not because its author is careless – on the contrary, they keep their statements and honestly admit they can’t guarantee anything. But because in a few sentences it gathers almost all of the typical mistakes:

  1. Listing is not execution. The law does not ask where the security is listed, but where the specific sale was concluded. The same share or ETF can be admitted to trading on a regulated market and at the same time be sold over the counter (OTC) – in which case there is no exemption.
  2. An ISIN is not a trading venue. The ISIN identifies the instrument, not the exchange – one ISIN trades on dozens of venues. The place of execution is shown by the MIC code in the trade confirmation, and specifically by the MIC code of the particular segment.
  3. A “regulated exchange” is not a “regulated market”. One exchange has segments with different licences. On Xetra, for example, the XETA segment (Regulierter Markt) is a regulated market, while XETB (Freiverkehr) is a multilateral trading facility (MTF). A sale through XETB is not a sale on a regulated market within the meaning of the law, even though “the exchange is Xetra”.
  4. The type of instrument matters too. The exemption covers a closed list of instruments: units and shares of collective investment schemes and national investment funds, shares, rights and government securities. ETNs and ETCs, which are debt instruments, are not on the list – even if they trade on a regulated market.
  5. “No problems so far” is not evidence. The absence of an audit does not confirm the tax treatment – it only postpones it.

The source of the claim, according to its author, was artificial intelligence. We will come back to that at the end. As for the question itself, the answer is: turning off routing does not solve anything on its own. What decides it is the venue where each sale was actually executed, and what the broker can document.

What the law says – in brief

Individuals (PITA)

  • Art. 13(1)(3): income from the disposal of financial instruments within the meaning of § 1(11) of the Supplementary Provisions is not taxable.
  • § 1(11)(a) of the Supplementary Provisions (as worded from 1 January 2026): transactions in units and shares of collective investment schemes and national investment funds, shares, rights and government securities, carried out on a regulated market and/or an SME growth market.
  • § 1(65) and (66) of the Supplementary Provisions (new, State Gazette No. 113/2025): a “regulated market” is one under § 1(70) of the Supplementary Provisions of CITA, i.e. under Art. 152(1) and (2) of the Markets in Financial Instruments Act (MFIA) – a system licensed and operating as a regulated market under Directive 2014/65/EU (MiFID II). An “SME growth market” is an MTF under Art. 122(1) MFIA and the corresponding markets in the EU.

The practical takeaway: for individuals, the exemption applies only to a regulated market or SME growth market in the EU/EEA. Markets in third countries are not covered. An NRA opinion (Plovdiv, 15 April 2024) points the same way: sales on US markets are taxable under Art. 33(3) PITA.

Companies (CITA)

  • Art. 44: the gain from disposals of financial instruments under § 1(21) of the Supplementary Provisions reduces the accounting financial result (para. 1), while the loss from such transactions increases it (para. 2). The exempt gain is not taxed, but the loss from the same transactions is not recognised either.
  • § 1(21) of the Supplementary Provisions: contains the same cases as PITA plus letter (e) (State Gazette No. 104/2020, in force from 1 January 2021) – transactions in shares carried out on a third-country market that is considered equivalent to a regulated market and for which the European Commission has adopted an equivalence decision under Directive 2014/65/EU.
  • Art. 196: exempts the income from the same transactions from withholding tax, but it applies to foreign persons with Bulgarian-source income. For a Bulgarian company, Art. 44 is what applies.

Five differences between a personal and a company account

1. Losses: an annual result versus a five-year horizon

Individual. Under Art. 33(3) PITA, taxable income from the sale of financial assets is the sum of the gains realised during the year, less the sum of the losses realised during the year. The result is “closed” on 31 December – an uncovered loss is not carried forward. Hence the practice of tax-loss harvesting: selling losing positions before year-end to offset gains already realised.

Company. Under Art. 70 CITA, a tax loss is carried forward and deducted over the following 5 years. A company has no need to realise losses at all costs before 31 December – this year’s loss will reduce the profit in the years that follow. Two caveats: the loss on transactions exempt under Art. 44 does not count (it is added back to the result), and the carried-forward tax loss is deducted from the company’s entire taxable profit, not just from trading.

2. Third-country markets: wider, but narrower than often claimed

Only CITA has letter (e). The Commission’s decisions under Art. 25(4)(a) of MiFID II date from 13 December 2017: (EU) 2017/2318 for Australia, (EU) 2017/2319 for Hong Kong and (EU) 2017/2320 for the United States. The Commission’s overview table of equivalence decisions (January 2026 update) lists only these three jurisdictions under that provision. Singapore, Canada and the United Kingdom have no such decision. The recognition for Switzerland was temporary and is no longer in force.

Ordinance No. 55 of the Financial Supervision Commission (FSC) on determining equivalent regulated markets also refers to the Commission’s decisions under the same provision. In other words, the “list” is not stand-alone – it follows the Commission’s decisions. Three limitations follow directly from the text:

  • Shares only. Letter (e) speaks of transactions “in shares”. In letter (a) of the same point, units and shares of collective investment schemes are listed separately from shares – which is why selling a US ETF, bonds or derivatives on a US market is in the risk zone and more likely taxable for the company too.
  • Venues are recognised in full, not by segment. The US decision names the national securities exchanges and the alternative trading systems (ATS) registered with the SEC. Unlike in the EU, there is no segment to look for here. But a trade executed outside these venues – internalised by a market maker or OTC – is not a trade on an equivalent market.
  • The burden of proof remains. For third-country markets too, the key document is the trade confirmation showing the code of the actual execution venue, checked against the annex to the Commission’s decision.

3. Expenses: a statutory 10% versus actual documented costs

Individual. Since 2024, PITA allows a 10% statutory expense deduction on taxable income from the sale of financial assets and currency trading. That is all. The accountant, filing the return, subscriptions to signal groups, bots, courses, analysis platforms and AI tools are not deductible separately, however much they cost.

Company. Under Art. 10 and Art. 26(1) and (2) CITA, any expense that is related to the business and documented is recognised: brokerage commissions and fees, accounting, data and signals, software and bots, training, AI. The caveats:

  • The expense must be the company’s own and for its business. The owner’s personal expenses are not recognised and may be treated as a hidden profit distribution (Art. 26(11) CITA).
  • VAT. Most of these services come from foreign suppliers. A company not registered for VAT must register under Art. 97a of the VAT Act when it receives such services. Since transactions in securities are exempt supplies (Art. 46 of the VAT Act), there is no right to input tax credit – the VAT effectively becomes a cost.
  • Fixed costs. Accounting, annual financial statements, an LEI code (without one, a legal entity cannot trade financial instruments), bank fees. With a small portfolio, these can exceed the tax saving.

4. Taking the money out: the second step that is often overlooked

The company’s profit is not the owner’s profit. When a dividend is distributed to a resident individual, a final tax of 5% is due (Art. 46(3) PITA; the increase to 10% proposed in the draft 2026 budget was not adopted). The arithmetic looks like this:

  • taxable profit in the company: 10% corporate tax + 5% on the remainder = 14.5% effective;
  • profit exempt under Art. 44: 0% in the company + 5% on distribution = 5%;
  • individual: 10% on 90% of taxable income = 9% effective, and 0% on a sale on an EU regulated market.

The conclusion is inconvenient for popular opinion: for an investor who sells ETFs on a regulated segment in the EU, a personal account is more advantageous than a company one. A company wins where there are taxable transactions, trading on markets in the US, Australia or Hong Kong, significant actual expenses, and losses that need to be carried forward.

Dividends received by the company itself are also treated differently: those from companies resident in the EU/EEA are not recognised for tax purposes (Art. 27(1)(1) CITA), while those from third countries are taxed at 10% with a credit for the tax paid abroad up to the amount of the Bulgarian tax (Art. 14 CITA).

5. Inheritance: when the portfolio is scattered around the world

In Bulgaria. Under Art. 29(1) of the Local Taxes and Fees Act (LTFA), where the deceased is a Bulgarian citizen, inheritance tax applies to inherited property both in Bulgaria and abroad. The surviving spouse and heirs in the direct line pay no tax; other heirs pay at rates set by the municipal council.

Abroad. This is where the trap lies. The US levies federal estate tax on deceased persons who are neither US citizens nor US residents on their US-situs assets. Among these, the IRS expressly lists shares in companies incorporated under US law – even if the certificates are held abroad or in the name of a nominee (the broker). Where these assets exceed USD 60,000, Form 706-NA must be filed. Bulgaria has no estate tax treaty with the US – it does not appear on the IRS list.

With a company. What is inherited are the shares in the Bulgarian company, i.e. property in Bulgaria. The US shares belong to the company, not to the deceased. This is not an automatic solution: the articles of association must regulate what happens to the shares when a partner dies, and the shares are valued for LTFA purposes. For individuals, a partial solution is funds domiciled in Europe (UCITS), which are not shares of US companies.

Why artificial intelligence sounds credible – and where the real problem is

Language models generate the most plausible text, not a verified reading of the law. In everyday speech, “regulated exchange” and “regulated market” are synonyms – and the model mixes them up exactly the way people do. It also often works with outdated versions (§ 1(11) PITA changed on 1 January 2026) and does not know which segment a particular broker used.

The most dangerous part is that the wrong answer sounds confident and comes dressed in correct details – ISIN, Appendix No. 5, a specific exchange. These details create a sense of expertise without proving anything.

But the problem often starts with the question itself. “Is it true that if the ETF is listed on Xetra, the sale is exempt?” contains a false premise – that listing is the criterion. A model is more likely to build on the premise than to challenge it. Also missing are a role, jurisdiction, date, instrument type, the actual execution venue and – most importantly – the legal text itself.

Here is a sample prompt that gets the best possible result even out of a free version of a publicly available AI. The key is that you put the law into the prompt yourself, rather than relying on the model to “remember” it:

Prompt
ROLE: Act as a tax adviser on Bulgarian law. Answer only on the basis of the legal text I give you below.

CONTEXT:
- I am: [a Bulgarian tax-resident individual / a Bulgarian limited company (OOD)].
- Tax year: 2026.
- Broker: [name, country, which regulator licenses it].
- Instrument: [share / UCITS ETF / ETN / ETC], ISIN: [...].
- Sold on [date]. The execution venue in the trade confirmation is: [MIC code of the segment, e.g. XETA / XETB / XOFF, or "not stated"].

LEGAL TEXT (pasted verbatim from an official source):
[PITA: Art. 13(1)(3); § 1(11), (65) and (66) of the Supplementary Provisions; CITA: § 1(70) of the Supplementary Provisions; MFIA: Art. 152(1) and (2)]
[for a company – also CITA: Art. 44, § 1(21) of the Supplementary Provisions]

TASK:
1. Determine whether the gain is taxable by analysing SEPARATELY: (a) the type of instrument; (b) the place of execution, NOT the place of listing; (c) the status of the specific segment (MIC).
2. For each conclusion, quote verbatim the provision you rely on. If you have no text for a given statement, write "no source".
3. Do not assume the premises in my question are true. If my framing is wrong, say so in the first sentence.
4. List which documents I should request from the broker as evidence.
5. Finish with: confidence level (high/medium/low) and what a specialist should check.

DON'TS: do not use "usually" or "as a rule" without a source; do not mix the PITA regime with the CITA regime; do not make up article numbers, opinions or court cases.

Two extra checks: ask the same question with the premise reversed (“Why is this sale NOT exempt?”) and compare the answers. And always check the quoted texts against the official source – if the quote does not exist, the answer goes in the bin.

In practice: what to check

  • Download the trade confirmation for every sale and look for the column with the actual place of execution – not “Listing Exchange”. The code XOFF means off-exchange execution.
  • Check the segment’s MIC code in the ESMA register: Regulated Market or Multilateral Trading Facility.
  • Check the type of instrument: a fund (UCITS) or a debt security (ETN/ETC).
  • For a company trading on third-country markets: check whether the venue is in the annex to the Commission’s decision and whether the instrument is a share.
  • Before opening a company account, add up the fixed costs and the tax on taking the money out – not just the corporate tax.
  • If the broker does not state the segment, ask for it in writing. Without proof of the place of execution, the exemption is just an assumption.
